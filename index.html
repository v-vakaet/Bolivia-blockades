Critical Incident Log
May 1, 2026
ESCALATION
COB announces indefinite general strike. Transport syndicates join immediately. Blockades begin in La Paz and El Alto perimeter. Demands are initially economic.
May 6, 2026
SPREAD
ABC (Road Authority) reports 21 blockades on the national road network. La Paz–Oruro highway is now partially blocked at Patacamaya and Viacha.
May 11, 2026
SPREAD
38 blockade points reported by ABI on 6 departments simultaneously. Routes to Boyuibe, San Julián and Abapó (Santa Cruz) blocked for first time. Hundreds of trucks stranded.
May 13, 2026
TOURIST
Anna Enns, 56, tourist from Belize, dies at Desaguadero border. Ambulance blocked from reaching hospital. This is the first confirmed tourist death directly attributed to the blockades.
May 13, 2026
DEATH ×2
Second woman (40) dies in ambulance near Guanay. Third victim (20-year-old, El Alto) reported day after. All three deaths caused by blocked emergency access. International outcry begins.
May 14, 2026
TOURIST
U.S. Embassy Security Alert: "Do not travel by road between cities. Drivers attempting to pass roadblocks have reported violence, physical assaults, and car damage." Tourism body confirms tourists stranded across the country. Daily sector losses exceed USD 720,000.
May 16, 2026
MILITARY
Government deploys 2,500 police + 1,000 troops ("Corredor Humanitario" operation) to attempt to clear roads. Convoy makes partial progress but does not open La Paz–Oruro corridor fully.
May 18, 2026
VIOLENCE
Most violent day of the crisis: three converging marches clash with police in La Paz. Homemade explosives used, police vehicle burned, cable car station attacked, public offices ransacked, bystanders physically assaulted. Banks close branches citing security risks.
May 19, 2026
PRESS
15 journalists and 4 camera crews assaulted within one week, per the Defensoría del Pueblo. YPFB halts fuel deliveries as its Senkata plant is blockaded. Food shortages begin in La Paz (meat, poultry).
May 21, 2026
DEATH
4th death: 12-year-old boy dies after ambulance is blocked for 3+ hours. Counter-protests begin: citizen marches in La Paz, Cochabamba, Santa Cruz, Trinidad demand pacification.
May 23, 2026
VIOLENCE
Second humanitarian convoy attacked. 150-vehicle column (police, military, civilian trucks) pelted with stones and homemade explosives. Vehicles burned at Copata, Achica Arriba and Vilaque. 57 people detained. Convoy forced to retreat.
May 24, 2026
PEAK
59 blockade points — the highest count of the crisis, across 6 of 9 departments. Senate votes to repeal the State of Exception Law, limiting the government's tools for crisis response.
May 25, 2026
W4 DAY 4
58 blockade points remain. Protesters attempt to block access to El Alto international airport. No dialogue achieved — planned meeting with campesino leaders cancelled after Sunday's convoy failure. Potosí–Tupiza road confirmed open by police (evening).
May 26, 2026
FIELD REPORT
Potosí–Tupiza road reported blockaded around 00h30, contradicting the police report from the prior evening. This illustrates the core problem: conditions change within hours and no official report can be fully trusted.
May 26, 2026
NEW PEAK
62 blockade points — new all-time high, per ABC. Up from 54 in 24 hours. Potosí: 10 specific blockades listed including Yocalla–Tarapaya (on Uyuni corridor). Uyuni bus terminal confirms 3 consecutive weeks of partial service suspension.
May 26–27, 2026
SIGNED
President Paz signs and promulgates the abolition of Ley 1341 — published in the Gaceta Oficial hours after congressional passage. Military can now be deployed in civil conflicts without prior conditions. Government insists state of exception remains "last resort." Analysts warn the 2000 precedent shows such measures can strengthen, not dissolve, protest movements.
May 27, 2026
DIALOGUE
First formal dialogue session held at Bolivia's Vicepresidency, convened by VP Edmand Lara (himself an opponent of Paz). Present: govt ministers, Church, Defensoría del Pueblo, some social sectors. Absent: COB, campesino leaders from La Paz, rural teachers — many face arrest warrants. Sole agreement: government will guarantee safe passage for leaders to attend the next session. Follow-up meeting set for Thursday May 28, Seminario San Jerónimo, La Paz. Blockades continue uninterrupted.
May 27, 2026
NEW PEAK
66 blockade points — new record per ABC. Distribution: La Paz 21 · Potosí 14 · Cochabamba 12 · Oruro 11 · Chuquisaca 4 · Santa Cruz 3. La Jornada (Mexico) reports up to 150 points when counting municipal and local roads in addition to the national network. Potosí's jump from 10 to 14 is directly relevant to the Tupiza–Uyuni tourist corridor.
May 28, 2026
TALKS FAIL
Second dialogue session collapses. Convened by the Bolivian Episcopal Conference, the meeting again proceeded without the two key protest leaders — COB's Mario Argollo and Túpac Katari's Vicente Salazar — despite government guarantees they would not be arrested. The COB conditions any attendance on the cancellation of arrest warrants (Argollo faces "incitement and terrorism" charges). President Paz had warned he was inviting them "for the last time." Mediators now seek a formal lifting of the warrants.
May 28, 2026
CABILDO
In response, COB and Túpac Katari grassroots organisations call a national cabildo (mass popular assembly) to decide whether to continue the mobilisation — raising the prospect of a hardening rather than a resolution. 64 blockades active (La Paz 22, Potosí 15). Public transport drivers add a separate "indefinite strike," now in its 2nd day, over fuel-quality compensation.
May 28, 2026
HEALTH
Humanitarian situation deteriorating sharply. The Medical College reports blood reserves are "at the limit." Hospitals face critical shortages of oxygen and medicine. The El Alto municipality estimates daily losses of $6.5 million. Government says a state of exception remains "an instrument to be evaluated by opportunity and need" — neither ruled in nor out.
May 29, 2026
BREAKTHROUGH?
Arrest warrants against the main protest leaders cancelled. The key obstacle to dialogue — the aprehensión orders against COB leader Mario Argollo and Túpac Katari's Vicente Salazar — were lifted, removing their stated precondition for sitting at the table. Blockades nonetheless rose to 76 that morning; medical staff marched demanding a "humanitarian pause." The opening is real but fragile.
May 30, 2026
RECORD ↑
93 blockade points — a new record across six departments (La Paz 22, Cochabamba 21, Oruro 19, Potosí 15, Chuquisaca 11, Santa Cruz 5). The protests reach the one-month mark. A decisive ampliado nacional (mass assembly) is called for 14h00 Saturday in La Paz to decide whether to negotiate or escalate — with talks expected to follow on Sunday if the bases agree. The next 48 hours are pivotal.
May 31, 2026
DIALOGUE OFF
The COB assembly rejects dialogue and votes to keep the blockades until President Paz resigns — even though the courts had lifted the arrest warrant against COB leader Mario Argollo two days earlier, which had been the movement's stated precondition. The government's planned Sunday negotiating table was suspended indefinitely. The diplomatic opening has closed. The sole softening: the COB announced "humanitarian corridors" to let medicine, food and ambulances pass — but stressed this is not a relaxation of the protest.
Jun 1, 2026
DAY 32
Blockades hold at ~90 points across seven departments as the crisis enters its second month with no negotiation in prospect. President Paz declares a "truce" and asks the movement for "democratic maturity," while saying a state of exception with the military remains a last resort. Humanitarian corridors are reported functioning unevenly. For travellers, the practical situation is unchanged: the altiplano remains impassable by road and air travel is the only reliable option.
Jun 2, 2026
CABILDO
A multi-sector cabildo in El Alto reaffirms the blockades until Paz resigns. Convened by the COB and the neighbourhood federations (Fejuve), it set aside earlier sector-specific demands to focus solely on the president's departure. The La Paz governorate declared a 90-day health and humanitarian emergency over shortages of medicine, oxygen, food and fuel. A revocatory-referendum idea floated by some legislators was dismissed as constitutionally impossible — Paz has been in office only six months.
Jun 3, 2026
DAY 34
Three cabinet ministers put their resignations at the president's disposal as the political pressure on the government mounts. President Paz sent Congress a bill to regulate states of exception and accused groups linked to drug trafficking of helping finance the protests — a claim the movement rejects. The death toll attributed to the crisis is now reported at nine, six of them people who could not reach medical care because of the blockades. No change for travellers: highland road travel remains off-limits; fly and reconfirm same-day.
Jun 4, 2026
DAY 35
The transport confederation's 48-hour ultimatum expires today, with the truckers warning of joint new pressure measures if the government fails to act — adding another sector to the stand-off. Blockades hold around 92 points across seven departments, Cochabamba (31) and La Paz (20) the worst. The Ombudsman has warned that confrontational language from government spokespeople is undermining mediation efforts. For travellers the picture is unchanged: avoid all highland road travel, treat Santa Cruz and Tarija as the safer anchors, and rely on flights.
Traveller Risk Assessment
Active Risks to Travellers
HIGH
Getting stranded for days. Blockades appear and disappear within hours with no warning. Even routes confirmed "open" by authorities can close overnight (Potosí–Tupiza, May 25→26).
HIGH
Physical assault at blockade points. US Embassy explicitly documented "violence, physical assaults and car damage" against drivers attempting to pass. Not directed at tourists specifically, but indiscriminate.
HIGH
Medical emergency without access. At least five people have died because ambulances were blocked or amid clearing operations. Any health incident on the road carries serious risk, and hospitals now face oxygen, blood and medicine shortages.
MED
Airport access disruption. Protesters targeted El Alto airport access on May 25. The airport itself is open; the road to it is a risk in the La Paz area.
MED
Caught in urban unrest. Central La Paz, El Alto perimeter, and areas near government buildings have been sites of explosive use, fires, and crowd clashes.
LOW
Direct targeting of travellers. No evidence found in any Spanish-language source of protesters deliberately targeting foreign visitors as such. The danger is collateral, not ideological.

⚑ HISTORICAL ANALOGY — CNN ESPAÑOL & LOCAL ANALYSTS The crisis is being directly compared to October 2003, when similar road blockades, mounting deaths and irreconcilable demands forced the resignation of President Gonzalo Sánchez de Lozada — Bolivia's last comparable crisis. That standoff lasted 3–4 weeks before the president fell. CNN's analyst noted: the current situation lacks that crisis's unified leadership, which means it could be harder to resolve — no single negotiating partner can deliver a binding agreement even if Paz concedes.
La Paz
& El Alto
⛔ AVOID — highest-risk zone. The seat of government is encircled by the most blockades (22 points) and has seen the worst violence: explosives, burned vehicles, assaults on bystanders and journalists. Food, fuel, oxygen and medicine are critically short. Protesters have intermittently targeted access to El Alto international airport. If transiting by air, minimise time on the ground and do not move around the city by road.
Altiplano core
Oruro · Cochabamba
⛔ AVOID by road. These are the most densely blockaded departments (see heatmap). The La Paz–Oruro–Cochabamba highway axis is repeatedly cut, with hundreds of vehicles stranded for days. Two military clearing operations have already failed here. Mountainous terrain means few alternative routes.
Potosí · Uyuni
· Tupiza
⚡ VOLATILE — verify hour by hour. Potosí department holds 15 blockade points. The Uyuni salt flats and the towns themselves have generally remained accessible, and the Uyuni and Villazón (Argentine border) corridors have opened and closed repeatedly. Roads confirmed open by police have been blocked again within hours. Travel only on same-day local confirmation; never assume a road will stay open.
Santa Cruz
& eastern lowlands
✓ SAFEST REGION. Only 3 peripheral blockade points; the city and Viru Viru international airport operate normally. The surrounding lowlands (incl. Amboró) are largely unaffected. For travellers needing to wait out the crisis or exit internationally, this is the most reliable base.
Tarija · Beni
· Pando
✓ NO BLOCKADES REPORTED. The southern tip (Tarija, with functioning Argentine border crossings) and the northern Amazon departments are clear, though they sit off most standard tourist routes.
General guidance (4 June): Air travel remains the only reliable way to move between regions, and all major airports are operating — though airport access roads, especially in La Paz, can be disrupted. Treat Santa Cruz and the eastern lowlands as the safe anchor and treat the La Paz–Oruro–Cochabamba altiplano as off-limits by road. The COB rejected dialogue on 31 May and a 2 June cabildo reaffirmed the blockades (~90 points) with no agreement in sight — so no traveller should build plans around near-term improvement, and a forced clearance could raise tensions further. The single most important rule: do not attempt long-distance road travel through the highlands, and reconfirm any flight and airport-access status the same day you travel. Register with your embassy or consulate so you receive official alerts directly.
Trajectory: the stand-off has hardened and is now openly about Paz's survival. After peaking at a record 93 blockades on 30 May, the count has held flat around 90–92 across seven departments. The COB rejected dialogue on 31 May, and a 2 June cabildo in El Alto reaffirmed that the blockades continue until the president resigns — explicitly dropping sector demands in favour of that single goal. On 3 June three ministers offered to resign, and the transport unions' ultimatum to the government expires on 4 June — both signs the pressure is still building.
What comes next: with negotiation rejected and the demand narrowed to resignation, the plausible paths are (1) the blockades grind on, deepening the food, fuel, oxygen and medicine shortages until economic and political exhaustion forces a resolution, or (2) Paz uses force to clear routes — and on 3 June he sent Congress a bill to regulate states of exception, the legal groundwork for a military intervention. That path risks a sharp, possibly violent escalation, and two earlier clearing operations already failed. Paz has paired this with calls for a "truce" and an accusation that narco-linked groups are funding the protests, but the movement has shown no sign of standing down. "Humanitarian corridors" for medicine and ambulances are the one concession, and they function unevenly.
Humanitarian backdrop: worsening regardless of the politics. The death toll attributed to the crisis is now reported at nine, six of them people who could not reach medical care because of the blockades. La Paz department has declared a 90-day health and humanitarian emergency; hospitals are short of oxygen and medicine, and basic goods in La Paz and El Alto have doubled or tripled in price where they can be found at all.
For travellers: the eastern lowlands (Santa Cruz) and the far south (Tarija) remain the most navigable areas, and air travel between regions still functions. The La Paz–Oruro–Cochabamba altiplano is the danger zone and should not be crossed by road. With no diplomatic breakthrough in sight, the prudent posture is to avoid highland road travel entirely, keep to regions with functioning airports, and reconfirm flights and airport access on the day of travel rather than relying on conditions reported even a few hours earlier.
